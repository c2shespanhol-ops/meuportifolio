## 5. Metric selection framework

A metric should answer a product question. D15 uses five filters:

1. **Question:** what do we need to understand?
2. **User behavior:** what behavior would reveal the answer?
3. **Signal:** what measurable or observable indicator represents that behavior?
4. **Outcome:** what user or business outcome should change?
5. **Decision:** what would we do differently depending on the result?

This prevents choosing metrics simply because they are easy to collect.

## 6. Metric hierarchy

D15 distinguishes three levels:

### Outcome metrics

Indicate whether the intended user or business result is improving.

Examples for the reconstructed Habitat scenario:

- recurring demand rate;
- successful self-service completion;
- avoidable support dependency.

### Behavioral/product signals

Explain what may be driving the outcome.

Examples:

- content usage;
- repeated access to the same guidance;
- persistent doubts;
- journey abandonment or hesitation;
- frequency of direct support after content exposure.

### Operational metrics

Help understand the efficiency of the supporting operation.

Examples:

- average resolution time;
- volume of recurring requests;
- rework associated with incomplete information.

The distinction matters because an operational metric can improve without the user outcome improving.

## 7. Metric tree for the practice case

### Desired outcome

**Increase user autonomy in recurring SaaS journeys while reducing avoidable support dependency.**

### Outcome indicators

- recurring support demand;
- successful completion without direct support.

### Diagnostic signals

- usage of guidance;
- persistent doubts;
- repeated contacts after guidance;
- friction points in the journey.

### Operational indicators

- average resolution time;
- support volume;
- rework generated by incomplete or unclear information.

The tree is a reconstructed analytical model, not a historical Habitat KPI tree.

## 8. Interpreting metrics

A Product Owner should not treat a metric as an automatic explanation.

D15 applies four questions:

- **What changed?**
- **Where did it change?**
- **For whom did it change?**
- **What plausible explanation should we investigate?**

A change in average resolution time, for example, may reflect different demand mix, process changes, staffing, user behavior or content improvements. The metric alone does not prove causality.

## 9. Segmentation

The same metric can hide materially different behaviors.

For Habitat, the practice should distinguish at minimum:

- doctors using the site;
- room owners using the site;
- clinic owners using the SaaS.

The SaaS metric set should therefore focus on clinic-owner journeys rather than mixing users who do not use the same product.

This reinforces the persona/product distinction established in D11 and D14.

## 10. Metric → decision examples

### Decision 1

Recurring support demand remains high after a guide is published.

**Interpretation:** the content may not be solving the underlying friction, or the selected problem may be broader than initially understood.

**Practice response:** investigate persistent doubts and journey friction before producing more content.

### Decision 2

Content usage is high but support dependency does not fall.

**Interpretation:** access does not equal successful task completion.

**Practice response:** investigate whether users can actually execute the journey after consuming the content.

### Decision 3

Resolution time falls while recurring demand remains stable.

**Interpretation:** operational efficiency may have improved without solving the user problem.

**Practice response:** avoid declaring product success from the operational metric alone.

### Decision 4

One persona shows materially different friction from another.

**Interpretation:** a generic experience may be hiding context-specific needs.

**Practice response:** segment the problem and adapt the journey/guidance to the relevant persona.

## 11. Causality and metric discipline

D15 explicitly avoids the common mistake of assuming:

**Metric moved → our initiative caused the movement.**

A stronger reasoning chain is:

**Metric moved → identify affected segment → inspect related signals → investigate plausible causes → test explanation → decide → observe subsequent outcome.**

Historical results from Habitat, including 27% fewer recurring demands and 31% lower average resolution time, remain observed outcomes. D15 does not attribute those results exclusively to the reconstructed product strategy.

## 12. North Star reasoning

D15 does not force a North Star Metric onto the historical experience.

For the reconstructed practice, the candidate north-star direction is **successful user autonomy in recurring SaaS journeys**, because it is closer to the desired product outcome than documentation volume or support productivity.

This is a practice hypothesis, not a historical Habitat metric.

## 13. Analytics instrumentation thinking

A Product Owner should also ask whether the required signal is actually observable.

For each desired metric, identify:

- event or behavior to observe;
- user/persona context;
- product/journey context;
- time window;
- baseline;
- comparison segment;
- data source;
- known limitations.

D15 does not claim that the user historically designed formal event instrumentation or an analytics stack.

## 14. Data quality and limitations

Metrics can mislead when:

- definitions change;
- populations are mixed;
- sample size is too small;
- a metric is used without a baseline;
- operational activity is mistaken for user value;
- correlation is treated as causation;
- qualitative evidence is ignored;
- missing data is treated as zero.

The Product Owner responsibility exercised here is analytical discipline, not statistical ownership.
