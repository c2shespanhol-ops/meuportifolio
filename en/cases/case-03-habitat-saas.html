CASE 03 · HABITAT CONSULTÓRIOS INTELIGENTES

From individual support to self-service.

A cross-functional experience that started in CX and user support and also involved Product and Scrum practices alongside new feature development, knowledge creation, operational improvement, chatbot support, direct involvement in the commercial journey, and content for different personas and product contexts.

My role: I worked at the interface between customers, product and development, bringing user evidence into Discovery, requirements, prioritization, validation and experimentation. Limite: final product priority remained with the product owner; I do not present this experience as formal ownership of Product Strategy or the entire Product Backlog.

The problem

Unstructured support

Support was handled through WhatsApp. There was no ticketing system or structured knowledge base.

Knowledge that was difficult to use

There were generic explanations about the system, but they assumed the user already knew the tool.

Support dependency

Recurring questions consumed operational time and could have been resolved by users themselves with appropriate guidance.

Misaligned expectations

Users sent messages outside business hours without knowing when they would receive a response.

Ecosystem and personas

Habitat did not treat all users as if they had the same needs. The ecosystem included different profiles and usage contexts.

Physicians

They used the Habitat website to find and use care spaces. They did not use the SaaS.

Room owners

They also used the Habitat website, with a journey different from physicians. The SaaS was not intended for this profile.

Clinic owners

When a customer had multiple rooms, the need was different. For this profile, Habitat provided the SaaS product to enable operational control.

Business logic

The SaaS offer was directed to the profile where the need and commercial return justified the product. It was not a solution commercially offered indiscriminately to all users.

This distinction guided knowledge creation: each piece of content was designed for the corresponding persona and journey. The content was not generic because product usage was not generic either.

What I did

01 · DISCOVERY

I observed the user's reality

I used support conversations, recurring questions, user behavior and real cases to identify usage problems, needs and friction points in the journey.

02 · PRIORITIZATION

I set the work order according to user value

There was a predefined list of topics, but I had autonomy to change their order based on customer need, recurrence, impact and difficulty observed in support. Priority determined which problems should first be turned into knowledge.

03 · REQUIREMENTS AND DECOMPOSITION

I turned needs into executable steps

When a need involved a larger journey, I broke the work into multiple layers and smaller steps. This happened, for example, in the Habitat website registration material and in SaaS-related documentation.

04 · UX AND BUILD

I structured content around the persona journey

I turned generic explanations into materials designed for the persona and how they used the product. The goal was to enable someone without prior knowledge to complete the journey clearly. Each guide had step-by-step instructions and, when applicable, a demonstration video.

05 · VALIDATION

I validated whether the solution worked in practice

Content production followed a structure that turned the user's need into concrete and verifiable steps. In addition to owner validation, I observed usage, remaining questions and request recurrence to identify points that needed correction or completion.

06 · SCALE

I turned knowledge into self-service

Videos were sent directly to users. Written content fed the chatbot and was also used in training.

The same solution began serving three needs: helping users, supporting the chatbot and training people internally.

Product and Scrum in practice

At Habitat, I wore multiple hats. In addition to CX, I worked at the interface between customers, product and development, participating in new feature development with the engineering team in a Scrum context. The team met weekly on Fridays.

Product Backlog

I worked with the list of demands and features and helped structure the work. Final decisions on new feature priority remained with the product owner.

Backlog Refinement

I helped the team understand, detail and clarify the work before development, reducing ambiguity around the customer's need.

Sprint Planning

I participated in defining the work, helping the team understand the customer's need and what had to be developed.

Daily Scrum

I participated in the meetings and, when I led the conversation, organized follow-up, identified blockers and helped route impediments.

Sprint Review

I participated in reviews of developed features, checking whether delivery met the need that originated the work.

Sprint Retrospective

I participated in identifying process problems and defining improvements for the next cycle.

The central point of my role: I brought the customer's real need into discussions with development and had the autonomy to say when a feature did not address the problem. In those cases, the delivery went back for adjustment.
I also influenced product decisions: in some situations, the owner's initial view of the user's need differed from what I observed directly with customers. I brought this evidence into the discussion and, in some cases, changed the perception of what should be considered.

This section describes practices actually performed. It does not imply that I was solely responsible for the Product Backlog, final prioritization, product strategy or all formal Product Owner or Scrum Master responsibilities.

From perception to evidence

An important part of my role was turning perceptions about what the customer needed into an evidence-based discussion.

01 · CUSTOMER INSIGHTS

I started from real behavior

Customer conversations, real cases, recurring questions, difficulties and observed behavior helped understand the problem before advocating a solution.

02 · EVIDENCE-BASED DECISION MAKING

I brought evidence into the decision

When my view of the customer's need differed from the owner's initial view, I brought concrete user examples and, when available, related those cases to the business metrics being monitored.

03 · VALUE-BASED PRIORITIZATION

I questioned what truly deserved attention

Recurrence, impact, user need, difficulty and potential for self-service helped distinguish more relevant problems from requests that only appeared important.

04 · STAKEHOLDER INFLUENCE

I influenced without being the final decision-maker

My role was not to replace the owner in the decision. It was to bring customer evidence into the conversation so the decision considered the real problem observed at the frontline.

An important lens: This reasoning is close to the principle behind approaches such as the Kano Method, which seeks to understand how different needs affect perceived user value. However, I did not formally use the Kano Method at Habitat and therefore do not present it as a methodology I applied during that period.

What the experience demonstrates is real practice in Customer Insights, Problem Discovery, value-based prioritization and evidence-based influence.

Customer experience and operations

WhatsApp Business

I configured an away message to inform users of service hours and set expectations for response.

User validation

After IT or Development work, I returned to the user and asked for a test. The solution was considered complete only after validation.

Continuous improvement

When content did not fully resolve a question, the need for correction or completion returned to operations.

From experience to conversion

My role also covered the commercial journey. I directly handled leads identified from user interest.

Behavior

We used ClickUp to observe interaction points and behavior on the website.

Lead

Users who showed interest and requested information were treated by me as leads in Pipedrive.

Follow-up

I made first contact, understood the need, presented the product, answered questions, followed up and monitored the negotiation.

Prioritization

Initial contact followed intake order. Afterwards, priority changed according to urgency, interest and negotiation stage.

When the owner questioned conversion bottlenecks, I brought real customer cases and related operational observations to the conversion figures being monitored.

From need to delivery

01 · UNDERSTAND THE USER

Three profiles, different needs

The platform served physicians, room owners and clinic owners. The work started from the questions and friction observed in each profile, avoiding treating the product as a single experience.

02 · STRUCTURE THE NEED

Reconstructed User Story

As a physician, I want to understand the characteristics, rules and conditions of use of each product, so I can identify which is appropriate for my professional moment and contract and use the product to reserve a room.

03 · ACCEPTANCE CRITERIA

What needed to be clear

Product name, purpose, career stage, price, compatible rooms, usage rules, cancellation deadline, how to use and purchase, check remaining balance and use the product to reserve a room.

04 · VALIDATE AND LEARN

UAT/OAT and experimentation

I participated in validation with business and technology and in A/B tests, monitoring user behavior and completion of the expected action to support decisions to continue, change or discard.

Evidence boundary: this case demonstrates concrete experience with User Stories, acceptance criteria, refinement/prioritization, UAT/OAT and experimentation. It does not turn this experience into a claim of formal Product Backlog or Product Strategy ownership.
