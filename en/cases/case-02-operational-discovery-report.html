CASE 02 · DOCUMENT 01 · DISCOVERY

Operational Discovery Report

English version of the original supporting document. The content preserves the scope, reported outcomes and evidence limitations of the source.

01 · Executive summary

Product governance to reduce operational lead time

This discovery and diagnosis report documents the work to structure the technology-request journey for employees of the Secretariat of Tax and Fiscal Management (SEGET), particularly taxpayer service and tax operations. Requests involving access, infrastructure, equipment and other technology needs arrived through decentralized channels, without a structured curation layer between employees and the Department of Information and Communication Technology (TIC).

The Undersecretariat of Planning and Modernization created a portal to centralize requests. Employees registered their needs through the portal, while the Undersecretariat took responsibility for curation, guidance, routing and follow-up. Jira supported ticket management, deadline monitoring and escalation.

Reported outcome: approximately 71% reduction in operational lead time, from around one week to generally up to 48 hours.

02 · Institutional context

Organizational setting

SEGET depended on systems, infrastructure and technology equipment to perform its activities. The greatest operational impact was concentrated in taxpayer service and tax operations, although technology needs affected the Secretariat's employees more broadly.

SEGET

Business area dependent on technology infrastructure and systems.

Planning and Modernization

Responsible for process modernization, request curation and journey governance.

TIC

Department responsible for the technical execution of technology requests.

03 · Initial diagnosis

Problem discovery

Before the change, technology requests had no single governed journey. Access requests, for example, were submitted without consistent follow-up. Generic requests to mirror another employee's login could result in permissions that did not match the actual duties of the requesting employee's department.

There was also no institutional deadline-based monitoring routine. When a request remained inactive, no structured mechanism triggered follow-up.

  • Low predictability of service timelines.
  • No centralized request history or consistent tracking.
  • Incomplete requests and rework.
  • Fragmented communication with TIC.
  • Risk of excessive permissions caused by profile mirroring.
04 · Operational investigation

Identifying the bottleneck

Closer monitoring showed that the problem was not limited to technical execution time. A meaningful interval elapsed while requests waited for movement, often depending on someone noticing that a deadline had passed. Follow-up became active: checking deadlines, requesting updates and recording ticket status rather than waiting for a spontaneous response from TIC.

05 · Service reference

Service Charter and decision criteria

The TIC Service Charter was requested to understand expected timelines and distinguish incidents from service requests. It became the reference for monitoring. When a deadline had passed and the situation justified it, a reclassification to incident was requested so that the request could receive treatment aligned with its nature.

07 · Operational support

Request curation

Curation involved more than forwarding tickets. When employees were unsure which ticket type to select, how to complete a form, what information to provide or which documents to attach, guidance was provided directly. When necessary, support was provided in person to explain the procedure and show how to submit the request correctly. The aim was to reduce ambiguity at intake rather than transfer communication problems to TIC.

08 · Documentation standards

Form standardization

Participation included defining completion standards and rules for recurring situations. For example, when an employee needed access to multiple application menus and the form did not provide enough space, a standard was created to attach screenshots and state that the requested access should match the items shown in the attachments. This reduced interpretation differences between the request and technical execution.

09 · Access governance

Security and functional need

The process improved permission governance. Instead of routinely mirroring another login, access requests were based on the duties performed in the employee's department. When an employee moved to another department, previous access was removed, new duties were identified and only the permissions needed for the new role were requested. This was not a formal Information Security project, but the process incorporated access-control and least-privilege principles.

10 · Technology support

Jira as a tracking mechanism

Jira was used by Planning and Modernization to manage tickets and track intake, status, deadlines, follow-up, required actions, responses and closure. The tool did not constitute the solution by itself. The solution was the governance process that used Jira as a control mechanism.

11 · Monitoring routine

Systematic follow-up

The monitoring routine used the TIC Service Charter as a reference, reviewed ticket status, contacted TIC when required and escalated when appropriate. An additional monitoring margin helped avoid premature follow-up. Requests were based on objective criteria, turning informal reminders into a predictable routine.

12 · Journey ownership

Focal-point role

The work acted as a focal point between employees and IT. Communication flowed in both directions: employee to focal point to IT, and IT to focal point to employee. When IT needed information from an employee, or an employee needed an update, the focal point coordinated the exchange. This role included curation, guidance, follow-up, escalation, validation and process improvement.

13 · Quality and delivery

Solution validation

The process did not end when IT marked a ticket as completed. The employee was informed that access had been released and asked to test it. Some tickets were marked complete even though access was incorrect, incomplete or only partially delivered. When this happened, TIC was contacted for correction. The employee tested again, and the request was considered complete only after validation.

14 · Before and after

Process evolution

Before

Decentralized requests, waiting without visibility, low predictability, potential rework and delayed responses.

After

Centralized portal, active curation, standardized information, Jira tracking, deadline monitoring, structured escalation and user validation.

The change was not merely a new channel. It changed who was responsible for coordinating the journey.

15 · Hypothesis validation

Validated hypotheses

  1. Active follow-upSystematic monitoring and follow-up triggers increased response speed.
  2. StandardizationForms and documentation provided clearer execution information.
  3. Single entry pointEmployees gained a clear reference for submitting and tracking needs.
  4. Objective criteriaThe Service Charter provided a reference for monitoring and escalation.
  5. Final validationTesting with employees helped identify and correct inconsistencies before closure.
16 · Reported impact

Project outcome

Approximately 71% reduction in lead time. The reported lead time for requests fell from approximately one week to generally up to 48 hours. The case also records centralized intake, standardized forms, objective criteria, active monitoring, structured escalation and user validation as process changes.

17 · Comparative view

Transformation dimensions

  • Intake: decentralized to centralized portal.
  • Curation: absent to Undersecretariat-led.
  • Management: reactive to active follow-up.
  • Tooling: no structured tracking to Jira.
  • Criteria: loosely defined to Service Charter reference.
  • Communication: fragmented to focal-point coordination.
  • Access: profile mirroring to functional need.
  • Validation: assumed at closure to employee testing.
18 · Product perspective

Strategic reading

This case demonstrates practices aligned with Product Owner work in service governance and operational flow. The contribution involved understanding the problem, organizing the journey, supporting prioritization, following execution, connecting employees and TIC, and validating outcomes. This description does not claim a formal Product Owner title on the project or end-to-end ownership of product strategy and backlog.

19 · Demonstrated capabilities

Competencies

20 · Main challenge

Culture change

The greatest challenge was cultural change. Creating a portal was not enough; employees needed to change how they requested help, documented needs and tracked progress. A new relationship also had to be established between operations and TIC, relying less on informal contacts and more on criteria, records, monitoring and clear responsibility for the journey.

21 · Institutional value

Key learning

Efficient processes do not begin with technology. They begin with understanding people's needs and building governance that can sustain decisions over time.
22 · Conclusion

Report closing

The project transformed a fragmented journey into a centralized, monitored and validated process. Employees gained a clear entry point, the Undersecretariat took responsibility for curation, TIC received better-structured requests, follow-up used objective criteria and solutions were validated by the people who needed them. The reported result was an approximately 71% reduction in lead time, alongside improved predictability and governance of the Secretariat's technology requests.

Evidence boundary: the report describes the practical contribution and reported outcomes. It does not claim formal Product Owner authority or exclusive causality for the measured improvement.
